What is a speaker one-sheet?

A speaker one-sheet (also called a speaker sheet or speaker one-pager) is a one-page summary of you as a speaker. It's the document you attach to a pitch email, hand to an organiser at an event, or send when a planner asks "do you have something I can share with my team?"

Speaker one-sheet vs speaker website vs media kit

People often mix these up. Each one does a different job:

Length One page One or more web pages Several pages or a folder
Format PDF or print Live web page PDF, ZIP or web page
Main reader Event planners and their bosses Planners, attendees, media Journalists and event marketing teams
Main job Get you shortlisted and forwarded Get you found and booked Help others promote you
Includes video? A link or QR code Your full reel, embedded Links and files

You need a one-sheet and a website. The one-sheet travels: it gets attached, printed and forwarded inside a company. The website is where the planner goes when they want to see more, especially your video.

Why event planners still ask for a speaker one-sheet

Planners rarely book a speaker alone. A conference organiser runs ideas past a committee. An HR manager has to get a director to sign off. A one-sheet is the thing they forward with "what do you think of this person?"

That's why a good speaker one-sheet:

  • Stands on its own. Someone who has never heard of you should understand it without your pitch email.
  • Scans in 30 seconds. Headings, short lines, numbers and white space.
  • Looks professional. It reflects on the planner who forwards it.
  • Makes the next step obvious. Email, phone and a link to your reel are easy to find.

If your one-sheet does those four things, it's working for you in meetings you'll never see.

The anatomy of a speaker one-sheet: 10 blocks

Here's the same example with each block numbered. The sections below explain what goes in each one and how to write it.

The ten blocks of a speaker one-sheet, numbered in the order a planner reads them.

1. A professional headshot

Use a recent, well-lit photo where your face is clear. It should look like you on the day you walk on stage. A natural, friendly expression works better than a stiff corporate portrait. If you have a good photo of you speaking, you can use it here or add it as a second image.

Tip: put the photo at the top, where the eye lands first. It turns a document into a person.

2. Your name and role

Your name, big. Under it, a short line about what you do and for whom: "Keynote speaker · Leadership trainer" plus a line like "Helping managers lead through mergers, restructures and new leadership."

Avoid a long list of titles. "Author, coach, consultant, speaker, podcaster, entrepreneur" tells the planner nothing about what you'll do for their audience.

3. Your one-line promise

This is the most important sentence on the page. It tells the planner what changes for their audience after your talk.

Compare:

Weak Strong
"Award-winning leadership speaker" "I train managers to lead through change, with tools they use on Monday morning."
"Inspiring audiences worldwide" "I help sales teams handle rejection and close more deals the same quarter."
"Passionate about wellbeing" "I show nurses how to protect their energy on 12-hour shifts."

The strong versions name the audience, the change and a result. Write ten versions of your promise and pick the one a planner could repeat to their boss.

4. Proof numbers

Three or four numbers you can stand behind, in a band across the page. Good options:

  • Talks or keynotes delivered
  • People reached
  • Average session rating
  • Companies or organisations you've worked with
  • A result your audiences get (like "87% still use a tool 30 days later")
  • Years of experience in your field

Use real numbers only. If a planner asks where a figure comes from, you should have an answer.

5. A short bio

60 to 100 words, written in the third person, focused on why you're credible on this topic. Skip your full career history. A planner needs to know what qualifies you and what you do now.

A simple structure: your relevant experience in one sentence, who you help today in one sentence, and how you work (or what makes your sessions different) in one sentence. Our guide on how to write a speaker bio planners can use has examples at different lengths.

6. Signature talks

Two or three talks, each with:

  • Format and length: "Keynote · 45 min" or "Workshop · Half day"
  • A title people would repeat: "Leading Through Change," not "Change Management Keynote"
  • One sentence on what the talk is about
  • Three short takeaways

This is the block planners read most closely, because it tells them whether you fit their theme. Three talks is the maximum. If you have more, put the rest on your website.

7. Testimonials

One or two short quotes from people who booked you, with full names, roles and organisations. "Laura Bennett, Head of Learning, Meridian Group" is convincing. "A happy client" isn't.

The best testimonials mention a specific result: "Weeks later they were still using her one-message rule." If you don't have quotes like that yet, our guide on how to ask for testimonials right after an event will help.

8. Logos, formats and logistics

Two small blocks that answer practical questions before the planner has to ask:

  • Trusted by: five or six organisations you've spoken for, as names or logos (only with permission for logos).
  • Formats and audiences: keynote, workshop, virtual, panel; who you speak to; group sizes; where you're based and where you travel; how far ahead to book.

Fees are optional. Many speakers leave them off the one-sheet and discuss them per event, or add "Fees from..." if they want to filter out small budgets.

9. A clear call to action

A short line telling the planner what to do next, with your contact details: "Book Amara for your next leadership event," then email, phone and website. Put it at the bottom, where people finish reading, in a contrasting band so it stands out.

Add how quickly you reply ("Replies within 2 working days"). It's a small detail that makes you look easy to work with.

10. A QR code or link to your video

A one-sheet can't play video, but it can send people to it. Add a QR code that opens your speaker reel or website, with a label like "Scan to watch the speaker reel." On a PDF, also make your website address a clickable link.

Don't have a reel? Our guide on how to make a speaker reel from footage you already have walks through it.

How to create a speaker one-sheet for free, step by step

Step 1: Gather your content

Before you open any design tool, collect everything in one document:

  • Your best headshot and, if you have one, a stage photo
  • Your promise, written ten ways, with the best one picked
  • Three or four proof numbers
  • A 60 to 100 word bio
  • Two or three talks with takeaways
  • One or two testimonials with full names and roles
  • A list of organisations you've spoken for
  • Formats, audiences, location, travel and contact details
  • The link to your reel or website

Writing the content first is the step most speakers skip, and it's why so many one-sheets end up with gaps filled by vague phrases.

Step 2: Pick a free tool

Canva Design-led layouts, ready templates, easy PDF export Free plan works
Google Docs Simple, clean layouts, easy to update Free

Canva is the easiest choice for most speakers. Search its template library for "speaker one-sheet" or "one-pager" and choose a simple layout, or start blank and copy the structure from our example.

Step 3: Set up the page

  • Size: US Letter (8.5 × 11 in) for North America, A4 elsewhere. If you speak internationally, Letter or A4 both print fine on the other.
  • Margins: at least 0.4 inches (about 10 mm) so nothing gets cut off when printed.
  • Grid: a header band across the top, then two columns. Talks and bio on the wider left column, proof and logistics on the right.
  • Colours: one main colour and one accent, ideally the same as your website.
  • Fonts: one font family, two or three sizes. Headlines around 24 to 40 pt, body text 10 to 12 pt.

Step 4: Lay out the ten blocks

Place the blocks in reading order: headshot, name and promise across the top; proof numbers as a band underneath; bio and talks on the left; testimonials, logos and logistics on the right; call to action and QR code in a band at the bottom.

Leave white space between blocks. A crowded one-sheet looks like you couldn't decide what mattered.

Step 5: Add your QR code and links

Create a QR code that links to your speaker reel or website. Most design tools have a free QR code feature (Canva has one built in). Test it with your phone before you export. Then make your email and website clickable in the PDF.

Step 6: Proofread and test

  • Read every line out loud. Cut anything that doesn't help a planner decide.
  • Check names, titles and organisations in testimonials.
  • Print it once. Things that look fine on a screen can be too small on paper.
  • Open the PDF on your phone. Many planners will.
  • Ask someone who doesn't know your work to read it for 30 seconds, then tell you what you speak about. If they can't, rewrite your promise and talks.

Step 7: Export and name the file

Export as a PDF, ideally under 2 MB so it doesn't get blocked by email filters. Name it clearly: "Amara-Okafor-Speaker-One-Sheet.pdf," not "final-v3.pdf." The file name is the first thing a planner sees in their downloads.

How to use your speaker one-sheet

A one-sheet only works if you use it. Good places:

  • Pitch emails: attach it, but put your promise, one talk and your reel link in the email body too. Many planners won't open attachments from people they don't know.
  • Follow-ups: after a call, send it so the planner can forward it internally.
  • Events: bring a few printed copies when you speak, and hand them to organisers in the audience.
  • Your website: add a "Download one-sheet" link or button next to your booking form.
  • Speaker directories and bureaus: some ask for one when you apply.
  • LinkedIn: add it to the Featured section of your profile.

Update it every few months: new numbers, a fresh testimonial, a new talk. An out-of-date one-sheet is a quiet sign that you're not speaking much.

Common speaker one-sheet mistakes

  • Cramming in everything. Your whole CV, every talk you've ever given, five testimonials. Pick the best and cut the rest.
  • No promise. The page says who you are but not what changes for the audience.
  • Tiny text. Anything under 9 pt is hard to read when printed.
  • Anonymous testimonials. Without names, they look made up.
  • No link to video. Planners want to see you speak. Make it one scan or click away.
  • A busy background. Photos or patterns behind text make it harder to read.
  • Only a PDF. A one-sheet is a summary. Planners still need somewhere to go for more.

Frequently asked questions

What is a speaker one-sheet?

A speaker one-sheet is a one-page document that summarises you as a speaker: your photo, promise, proof, bio, talks, testimonials, clients, formats and contact details. Event planners use it to shortlist speakers and share options with their team.

What should be on a speaker one-sheet?

A speaker one-sheet should include a headshot, your name and role, a one-line promise, three or four proof numbers, a short bio, two or three signature talks, one or two named testimonials, client names, formats and logistics, a call to action with contact details and a QR code or link to your reel.

How do I make a speaker one-sheet for free?

Write your content first, then use a free tool like Canva, Google Docs or Word. Set up a one-page Letter or A4 layout, place the ten blocks in reading order, add a QR code to your reel, proofread, and export a PDF under 2 MB.

Should I include my speaking fee on my one-sheet?

It's optional. Many speakers leave fees off and discuss them per event. If you want to filter out small budgets, add "Fees from..." in the logistics block.

How long should a speaker one-sheet be?

One page. If you need more space, your content is too long. Move the extra detail to your speaker website and link to it.

What size should a speaker one-sheet be?

US Letter (8.5 × 11 inches) in North America and A4 in most other countries. Both print fine on either paper size if you keep margins of at least 0.4 inches.

Is a speaker one-sheet the same as a media kit?

No. A one-sheet is a single page for planners deciding whether to book you. A media kit is longer and helps journalists and event marketing teams promote you after you're booked.
